Gravesaw: black metal amp and cab for DI guitar and bass

Gravesaw is a VST3 amp with the cab built in: plug in a guitar or bass DI and choose a voicing between raw buzz and a thick wall. It comes from Code Chaos Audio, the plugin label of Polished mastering engineer Tim Borchert, and it targets problems that show up again and again in black metal premasters: fizz that turns painful once the track gets louder, low end that clouds the tremolo, and noise between the riffs.

Preparing a black metal demo mix with Gravesaw and Rimehall

This is the order that keeps a home recording easy to master. Everything happens in the mix, before anything goes to a mastering engineer.

  1. Record two rhythm takes, not one. Start from the presets Wall Rhythm L and Wall Rhythm R, pan the takes hard left and right, and try the wide L/R cab pair that Gravesaw includes for double-tracked rhythm guitars.
  2. Set the gate so the gaps stay silent. The gate sits in front of the amp (from -90 dBFS, which is off, up to -30 dBFS, with hold and hysteresis). Mastering compresses and limits, and any hiss between tremolo bursts comes up with it.
  3. Use Tight before you reach for EQ. Tight is a high-pass in front of the distortion, adjustable from 20 to 400 Hz. Less low end into the gain stage means a tighter riff and more room for bass and kick. For a doom bottom, keep it low.
  4. Decide on Fizz and the Tamed cabs on small speakers. Fizz shapes the top end above about 4 kHz. Six of the 19 cabs are harshness-tamed versions, for bright pickups and fizzy settings.
  5. Run the bass DI through Gravesaw as well. The bass presets (Bass Grind, Bass Clank, Doom Bass Deep) and the three bass cabs give it grind. Low Blend mixes the clean DI low end back under the distortion, so the bottom stays solid.
  6. Then add space. Rimehall on the guitar bus puts the riff into a cold hall without burying it. Export the mix with 3 to 6 dB of headroom and no limiter on the master bus.

Why double-tracked guitars have to survive mono

Phones, Bluetooth speakers and many club and festival systems play your song in mono or close to it. Two separate performances panned left and right add up to a louder riff in the middle when they are summed. A single take that was copied, delayed or widened to fake a double can cancel parts of itself in mono, and the guitars suddenly sound thin and hollow.

A master cannot repair that, because the problem sits inside the mix. That is why the audit at Polished checks the stereo image and phase of every mix before mastering. Recording real double-tracks with matching tones is the cheapest fix there is.

Tool, mix or master?

For rehearsal demos and pre-production, a Gravesaw tone in a rough mix is all you need. When the songs go to Bandcamp, streaming or a label, a separate mastering pass with fresh ears pays off: loudness for each platform, harshness under control, low end checked in mono. Gravesaw FAQ

Does Gravesaw run on a Mac?
Yes, on Apple Silicon Macs (arm64) as VST3 and Standalone. There is no Intel build and no AU version, so Logic Pro is not supported. The macOS build is not signed or notarized: macOS blocks it at first, and you allow it under System Settings → Privacy & Security.
Can I use my own impulse responses?
Yes. Switch the cab off and load your own IR after Gravesaw. The 19 built-in cabs are the synthesised IRs of the Black Metal Tone Kit by Code Chaos Audio.
Is there a free trial?
Yes. The Gravesaw demo on Gumroad is the full plugin with all 16 presets; it plays a short noise burst every 45 seconds.
Does a good amp tone make mastering unnecessary?
No. Gravesaw shapes the guitar and bass inside your mix. Mastering works on the finished mix: loudness, tonal balance across the whole song, consistency across a release. A good tone makes that job easier, it does not replace it.
